WEEK 1

Executive Assistant Foundations

Understand the EA role, the CEO context, professional standards, priorities, and the difference between reactive support and proactive executive partnership.

LESSON 1

What an Executive Assistant Actually Does

📘 Teaching

An Executive Assistant is not simply someone who manages a calendar. The role protects an executive's time, attention, information, relationships, reputation, and ability to make decisions. Depending on the company, an EA may also coordinate projects, travel, meetings, research, documents, vendors, and special projects.

💼 CEO Scenario

A CEO has 40 new emails, three meeting conflicts, a board document waiting for review, a customer escalation, and a flight change. A strong EA makes the situation easier to understand and act on by triaging information, spotting consequences, preparing options, and tracking commitments.

✍️ Practice

Write a one-page description of the EA role in your own words. Separate tasks that are purely administrative from tasks that require judgment.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can explain how an EA creates leverage for a CEO without reducing the role to calendar management.

LESSON 2

CEO Context: How a Business Works

📘 Teaching

You cannot support a CEO well if you understand only the CEO's schedule. Learn the company's customers, offer, revenue model, major costs, departments, leadership roles, goals, and risks. Business context lets you understand why a request matters.

💼 CEO Scenario

A simple request for a meeting may involve an investor, a major customer, or a strategic partner. The scheduling task looks administrative, but the relationship may be business-critical.

✍️ Practice

Choose one real company and create a one-page business map: customers, offer, revenue, departments, leaders, and top risks.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can explain the basic business context behind the executive's priorities.

LESSON 3

Professional Standards and Executive Trust

📘 Teaching

Trust is the core currency of executive support. It is built through discretion, accuracy, consistency, preparedness, honesty, and calm behavior under pressure. Small habits compound into a reputation for reliability.

💼 CEO Scenario

If you create a scheduling mistake, hiding it creates more risk than reporting it early. A professional response states the problem, impact, options, and correction.

✍️ Practice

Create your personal EA operating principles for mistakes, confidential information, urgent requests, and uncertain instructions.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can describe trustworthy behavior in practical situations.

LESSON 4

Reactive vs. Proactive Support

📘 Teaching

Reactive support waits for instructions. Proactive support notices patterns and prepares before the executive asks. Proactivity does not mean making unauthorized decisions; it means removing preventable friction and presenting safe options.

💼 CEO Scenario

Before a board meeting, a proactive EA checks travel, attendee confirmations, agenda status, pre-reads, deadlines, room setup, and outstanding actions instead of waiting for the CEO to discover gaps.

✍️ Practice

Take one recurring responsibility and build a before/during/after checklist. Mark what can happen before someone asks.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You think one step ahead while respecting approval boundaries.

LESSON 5

The EA Control Center

📘 Teaching

A strong EA needs one reliable system for commitments, deadlines, follow-ups, recurring work, waiting items, and notes. Important work should not live only in your memory or scattered chats.

💼 CEO Scenario

A CEO says, 'Send Sarah the latest customer deck and remind me to call John next Tuesday.' Those become tracked commitments with owners, dates, and status.

✍️ Practice

Build a control center with Today, This Week, Waiting For, Upcoming, Recurring, and Private sections.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can quickly answer what needs attention and what is at risk.

WEEK 2

Executive Communication

Learn to communicate with CEOs, leadership teams, clients, vendors, and staff with clarity, brevity, professionalism, and appropriate escalation.

LESSON 1

Writing for Busy Executives

📘 Teaching

Executive communication is designed for fast comprehension. Put the purpose near the top, separate facts from recommendations, and make the requested action or decision obvious. Supporting detail can follow.

💼 CEO Scenario

Instead of a long scheduling paragraph, write: 'Decision needed: choose one of three vendor slots. My recommended option is Tuesday 2 PM because it avoids the leadership meeting.'

✍️ Practice

Rewrite five long messages into: context, action/decision, deadline, supporting detail.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can write concise messages without deleting information that changes the decision.

LESSON 2

Email Triage and Prioritization

📘 Teaching

Inbox management is information and decision management. Not every email deserves the same response time. Useful categories include urgent, CEO action, delegate, schedule, waiting, FYI, and noise.

💼 CEO Scenario

An investor message, employee emergency, customer escalation, and marketing newsletter should not all occupy the same priority level.

✍️ Practice

Create five to seven inbox categories and define a rule for each. Triage 25 sample emails.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can explain why a message was prioritized, delegated, scheduled, or archived.

LESSON 3

Tone, Diplomacy, and Stakeholder Management

📘 Teaching

When you write for an executive, your tone represents the executive and company. Diplomacy means being clear about limits without becoming dismissive. Offer alternatives when practical.

💼 CEO Scenario

Instead of 'The CEO is too busy,' say, 'The CEO is unable to attend that time, but can do Thursday at 3 PM or Friday at 10 AM.'

✍️ Practice

Write five versions of the same scheduling message: formal, warm, firm, apologetic, and executive-neutral.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can protect the executive's position while preserving goodwill.

LESSON 4

Escalation and When to Interrupt

📘 Teaching

One of the highest-value EA judgments is knowing what cannot wait. Consider safety, legal exposure, major financial exposure, reputation risk, strategic opportunity, deadline-critical issues, and decisions that only the CEO can make.

💼 CEO Scenario

A minor calendar conflict can wait for a briefing. A serious customer escalation or security event may require immediate escalation.

✍️ Practice

Create an escalation matrix with: interrupt now, escalate today, include in next briefing. Add examples.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can protect focus without filtering out critical issues.

LESSON 5

Verbal Briefings

📘 Teaching

A briefing transfers useful information quickly. A good structure is: what happened, why it matters, what happens next, and what decision or help is needed.

💼 CEO Scenario

A CEO asks, 'What's the launch status?' A strong answer is: 'On track for Friday. Design is complete, QA has one issue, and the team expects to close it by 3 PM. The payment integration is the only current risk.'

✍️ Practice

Practice five 60-second briefings from fictional business situations.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can give concise, accurate, decision-oriented updates.

WEEK 3

Calendar and Time Management

Master calendar architecture, scheduling judgment, buffers, time zones, recurring commitments, and executive energy management.

LESSON 1

Calendar as a Strategic Asset

📘 Teaching

A calendar is a resource allocation system. The EA helps make sure executive time reflects business priorities. That can mean protecting focus blocks, reducing unnecessary meetings, grouping similar calls, and leaving transition time.

💼 CEO Scenario

A CEO with eight hours of back-to-back meetings may look productive but have no time for thinking. Calendar design should account for the quality of attention, not just the number of meetings.

✍️ Practice

Audit a fictional two-week calendar and identify overload, missing preparation time, unnecessary fragmentation, and opportunities to consolidate.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can explain the business consequences of poor calendar design.

LESSON 2

Scheduling Requests

📘 Teaching

Scheduling involves attendees, time zones, urgency, duration, location, preparation, dependencies, and executive preference. The first available slot is not always the correct slot.

💼 CEO Scenario

For a sensitive customer meeting, the CEO may need 20 minutes of preparation and 15 minutes afterward. Booking exactly 30 minutes can create hidden operational cost.

✍️ Practice

Create a scheduling checklist for every external meeting request.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You consistently gather the information needed before proposing times.

LESSON 3

Time Zones

📘 Teaching

Time-zone errors create avoidable embarrassment. Confirm both location and time zone and use clear written labels. Remember that daylight-saving changes can alter offsets.

💼 CEO Scenario

A meeting that is 9 AM in Manila is not automatically 9 AM for a London or New York participant.

✍️ Practice

Create three cross-border schedules involving Manila, London, New York, Singapore, and Sydney.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can schedule international meetings accurately and communicate the time clearly.

LESSON 4

Buffers, Focus Blocks, and Energy

📘 Teaching

Not all hours have equal value. Executives need transition time, preparation, thinking time, meals, and breaks. A good schedule accounts for cognitive load.

💼 CEO Scenario

A high-stakes investor call followed immediately by a board presentation may technically fit but increase avoidable risk. A buffer can protect performance.

✍️ Practice

Redesign a 10-meeting CEO day by adding preparation, transition, focus, and meal time.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can defend protected time using business reasons.

LESSON 5

Recurring Meetings and Calendar Hygiene

📘 Teaching

Recurring meetings often survive long after their purpose disappears. Review recurring commitments, obsolete meetings, attendees, links, titles, and notes.

💼 CEO Scenario

An operations meeting that no longer requires the CEO may be delegated to another leader while the CEO receives a concise update.

✍️ Practice

Create a monthly recurring-meeting review checklist.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can keep the calendar purposeful rather than merely full.

WEEK 4

Inbox, Gatekeeping, and Relationship Management

Protect executive attention while maintaining strong relationships with customers, investors, employees, partners, and other stakeholders.

LESSON 1

The Executive Gatekeeper Role

📘 Teaching

Gatekeeping is thoughtful access management, not simply blocking people. The aim is to protect focus while ensuring high-value relationships receive appropriate attention.

💼 CEO Scenario

A cold sales message should not automatically receive the same treatment as a major customer, investor, board member, or employee emergency.

✍️ Practice

Create an access matrix: escalate, route, schedule if relevant, or decline politely.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can explain access decisions without becoming territorial or rude.

LESSON 2

Relationship Mapping

📘 Teaching

A CEO's network includes customers, investors, partners, advisors, employees, candidates, vendors, media, and personal contacts. A relationship map makes follow-up visible.

💼 CEO Scenario

After the CEO meets a potential partner, capture context, promised follow-up, and next step instead of simply archiving the conversation.

✍️ Practice

Build a stakeholder tracker with person, organization, relationship, last touch, next action, owner, and notes.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can turn relationship information into actionable follow-up.

LESSON 3

Waiting-For Management

📘 Teaching

Executives often lose momentum because they are waiting for someone else's response. Maintain a waiting-for list with the person, item, date promised, follow-up date, and escalation rule.

💼 CEO Scenario

If a supplier promised a proposal on Monday and it is Wednesday, the EA should know whether and how to nudge them.

✍️ Practice

Create a waiting-for tracker with 15 items and follow-up dates.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can surface stuck work before it becomes a larger problem.

LESSON 4

Personal vs Professional Support

📘 Teaching

Some CEOs expect their EA to coordinate personal logistics. The support level should be agreed, and private information should be handled carefully.

💼 CEO Scenario

Restaurant booking is low risk; handling family records, private financial information, or sensitive travel information requires stronger controls and clear boundaries.

✍️ Practice

Write a boundary guide for personal errands, family information, private data, and after-hours requests.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can support personal tasks without becoming careless about privacy or scope.

LESSON 5

Declining and Redirecting Requests

📘 Teaching

An EA often has to say no or not now. A professional redirect gives a useful alternative such as another owner, different time, shorter call, or written update.

💼 CEO Scenario

'The CEO cannot do that' is less useful than 'The CEO cannot meet this week; the COO can take the call Wednesday, or the CEO can do 20 minutes next Tuesday.'

✍️ Practice

Draft 10 polite redirect messages for common scenarios.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can protect time while preserving goodwill.

WEEK 5

Meetings, Agendas, Minutes, and Follow-Through

Run meetings as operational systems: clear purpose, prepared participants, decisions, actions, and follow-up.

LESSON 1

Meeting Design

📘 Teaching

A good meeting starts before anyone joins. Define the purpose: inform, discuss, decide, create, or unblock. Then determine who truly needs to attend and what outcome should exist at the end.

💼 CEO Scenario

A meeting with 12 people and no decision owner may produce discussion but no progress.

✍️ Practice

Convert five vague meeting requests into outcome-based meetings.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can design meetings around outcomes rather than habit.

LESSON 2

Agenda Writing

📘 Teaching

A useful agenda contains purpose, expected outcome, topics, time boxes, owners, and pre-reads.

💼 CEO Scenario

A leadership meeting might allocate time for metrics, risks, decisions, and actions rather than letting every topic expand indefinitely.

✍️ Practice

Create agendas for a 30-minute CEO one-on-one, leadership meeting, investor call, and customer escalation.

🎯 Mastery Standard

Participants know what to prepare and what decisions are expected.

LESSON 3

Useful Meeting Notes

📘 Teaching

Good notes capture decisions, action items, owners, deadlines, unresolved questions, and context. A transcript is not the same as useful minutes.

💼 CEO Scenario

Instead of recording every sentence, write: Decision, Owner, Deadline, Risk, Next checkpoint.

✍️ Practice

Take notes from a fictional meeting and turn them into a one-page action-and-decision summary.

🎯 Mastery Standard

Your notes are useful even to people who did not attend.

LESSON 4

Action Items and Accountability

📘 Teaching

An action item needs an owner and clear outcome. 'Follow up' is too vague unless you know with whom, about what, by when, and what completion looks like.

💼 CEO Scenario

'Ana to send revised pricing to Client X by 2 PM Friday' can be tracked and verified.

✍️ Practice

Rewrite 15 vague action items into measurable actions.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can convert discussion into accountable work.

LESSON 5

Meeting Follow-Up

📘 Teaching

Follow-up closes the operational loop. Send decisions quickly, save notes where the team can find them, and schedule checkpoints for important actions.

💼 CEO Scenario

After a leadership meeting, distribute decisions within an hour and place open actions into the project system.

✍️ Practice

Create a post-meeting SOP from meeting end through final follow-up.

🎯 Mastery Standard

Important decisions do not disappear after the call ends.

WEEK 6

Travel and Logistics Management

Plan business travel end-to-end, including itineraries, preferences, contingency planning, expenses, and executive briefs.

LESSON 1

Building an Executive Itinerary

📘 Teaching

An itinerary should answer where, when, how, with whom, what is needed, and what happens if the plan changes. It should be practical, not merely a list of booking confirmations.

💼 CEO Scenario

A useful itinerary contains flight, hotel, transfers, meeting addresses, contacts, local time, buffers, documents, and emergency details.

✍️ Practice

Build a two-day Manila-to-Singapore itinerary for a CEO with three meetings.

🎯 Mastery Standard

Your itinerary can be used without the executive reconstructing the trip.

LESSON 2

Travel Preferences and Profiles

📘 Teaching

Travel becomes faster when recurring preferences are documented: preferred airline, seat, hotel style, transfers, loyalty programs, and booking rules. Store them securely.

💼 CEO Scenario

Knowing a preferred early flight can remove unnecessary back-and-forth, while major decisions still need confirmation.

✍️ Practice

Create a fictional executive travel profile and preference checklist.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can apply preferences without confusing them with authorization.

LESSON 3

Contingency Planning

📘 Teaching

Travel fails. Flights are delayed, meetings move, bags are lost, and weather changes. Prepare alternatives for high-impact risks.

💼 CEO Scenario

For a critical presentation, you might have an alternate flight, backup transport, digital document copies, and local contacts saved.

✍️ Practice

Identify 10 travel risks and a contingency for each.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can respond quickly because likely failure points were considered in advance.

LESSON 4

Travel Expenses

📘 Teaching

Travel coordination often intersects with finance policy, receipts, reimbursement, and approvals. Learn the process without acting as the finance authority.

💼 CEO Scenario

Confirming invoice names, required receipts, or booking codes before travel can prevent later reimbursement problems.

✍️ Practice

Create a travel expense checklist from booking through reimbursement.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can coordinate with finance without bypassing policy.

LESSON 5

Pre-Trip Executive Brief

📘 Teaching

A concise pre-trip brief can include purpose, schedule, key contacts, meeting goals, documents, open decisions, and risks. It turns travel information into a decision aid.

💼 CEO Scenario

For a customer trip, include attendee names, relationship context, objectives, and promised follow-up.

✍️ Practice

Create a one-page pre-trip briefing template.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can help the CEO enter travel prepared rather than merely informed.

WEEK 7

Research, Briefings, and Document Management

Research quickly, evaluate sources, summarize accurately, and create executive-ready briefing documents.

LESSON 1

Researching for a CEO

📘 Teaching

Executive research is purpose-driven and time-bounded. Start with the decision or question, define what is needed, identify trustworthy sources, then stop when you have enough evidence.

💼 CEO Scenario

If the CEO is meeting a potential partner, useful research may include ownership, business model, leadership, recent developments, customers, and strategic relevance.

✍️ Practice

Create a research plan for a fictional partner meeting.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You research toward a decision instead of collecting trivia.

LESSON 2

Source Quality and Fact Checking

📘 Teaching

A primary source such as a company filing, contract, official page, or government document often deserves more weight than an unsourced summary. Check dates, context, and whether the evidence actually supports the claim.

💼 CEO Scenario

A recent official filing can be more useful than an old article repeating a number from years ago.

✍️ Practice

Take 10 fictional claims and label them verified, needs confirmation, opinion, or unsupported.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can distinguish evidence from assumption.

LESSON 3

Executive Briefing Memos

📘 Teaching

A briefing memo compresses information into a decision-friendly format: purpose, key facts, implications, options, risks, questions, and next steps.

💼 CEO Scenario

A vendor comparison might show cost, implementation time, security, contract terms, and operational impact.

✍️ Practice

Write a one-page briefing memo comparing three fictional vendors.

🎯 Mastery Standard

The reader can understand the situation quickly and see what requires attention.

LESSON 4

Version Control

📘 Teaching

Multiple unclear document versions create operational risk. Use clear names, dates, approved status, one source of truth, and sensible folders.

💼 CEO Scenario

'Board Deck FINAL final 2' is not a control system. A structured filename makes status visible.

✍️ Practice

Design a folder structure and naming standard for Board, Finance, Legal, HR, Travel, and Projects.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can retrieve the correct document quickly.

LESSON 5

Executive Document QA

📘 Teaching

Before a document reaches the CEO or an external party, check names, dates, numbers, links, formatting, page order, and consistency.

💼 CEO Scenario

A wrong company name in a proposal or wrong date in a board agenda can create reputational or operational problems.

✍️ Practice

Create a 20-point pre-send QA checklist.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You routinely catch preventable errors before they reach the executive.

WEEK 8

Project and Task Management

Coordinate projects, dependencies, owners, deadlines, risks, and status updates using practical project-management thinking.

LESSON 1

Projects vs Tasks

📘 Teaching

A task is a unit of work. A project is a coordinated outcome made of multiple tasks, people, dependencies, and deadlines. EAs often support projects even without the formal project-manager title.

💼 CEO Scenario

Launching an office is a project containing lease, vendors, budget, IT, access, equipment, and move-in tasks.

✍️ Practice

Classify five EA responsibilities as task, recurring workflow, or project.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can structure work at the right level.

LESSON 2

Owners, Dependencies, and Deadlines

📘 Teaching

Every important deliverable needs an owner and date. Dependencies show what must happen first.

💼 CEO Scenario

A website cannot launch until legal approves the terms and finance configures payments. Those dependencies need to be visible.

✍️ Practice

Map a fictional event project with 15 tasks, owners, deadlines, and dependencies.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can identify what is blocked and why.

LESSON 3

Status Reporting

📘 Teaching

A CEO status report should focus on what changed, what is on track, what is at risk, and what needs a decision. Activity lists alone can create noise.

💼 CEO Scenario

A useful report might say Green: on track; Amber: risk requiring attention; Red: blocked or likely to miss deadline, followed by reason and next step.

✍️ Practice

Create a one-page weekly status report for a fictional launch.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can report progress in business terms.

LESSON 4

Risk and Issue Logs

📘 Teaching

A risk is a possible future problem. An issue is a problem that has already happened. Both need owners and actions.

💼 CEO Scenario

Risk: supplier may miss delivery. Issue: supplier missed today's delivery.

✍️ Practice

Create a risk and issue log with 10 entries.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can keep uncertainty visible before it becomes a surprise.

LESSON 5

Closing Projects

📘 Teaching

Project closure includes confirming deliverables, documenting decisions, archiving files, reconciling expenses, capturing lessons, and retiring temporary tasks.

💼 CEO Scenario

After a major event, close vendor invoices, save the final attendee list, archive the runbook, and document improvements.

✍️ Practice

Create a project-closure checklist and lessons-learned template.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You finish projects cleanly instead of simply stopping work.

WEEK 9

Business and Financial Literacy

Develop enough financial and business literacy to follow CEO, CFO, board, budget, and performance discussions.

LESSON 1

Revenue, Cost, Profit, and Cash

📘 Teaching

Revenue is money earned from sales. Costs are resources consumed. Profit is a measure of revenue minus relevant expenses. Cash is money actually available to meet obligations. These are related but not identical.

💼 CEO Scenario

A company can show accounting profit and still experience cash pressure because money timing differs from accounting recognition.

✍️ Practice

Build a simple fictional monthly business model showing revenue, expenses, profit, and ending cash.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can follow basic business-finance conversations.

LESSON 2

P&L, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow

📘 Teaching

The P&L shows income and expenses over a period. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. Cash flow explains movements in cash.

💼 CEO Scenario

If a company buys equipment, the accounting impact and cash impact can differ.

✍️ Practice

Study a simplified fictional set of statements and write five observations.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You know which report answers which question.

LESSON 3

Budgets and Expense Controls

📘 Teaching

Budgets are planning tools. Expense controls define who may spend, what approval is required, and what documentation is needed.

💼 CEO Scenario

A CEO may approve a contract while finance still requires a purchase order before payment.

✍️ Practice

Create a fictional expense-approval workflow from request to payment.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You know where EA coordination ends and finance authority begins.

LESSON 4

Startup Metrics and Runway

📘 Teaching

Startup leaders may discuss MRR, ARR, churn, burn, runway, CAC, and LTV. You do not need to be a CFO, but you should understand common vocabulary.

💼 CEO Scenario

If monthly cash burn is high and cash reserves are limited, runway becomes an important planning concept.

✍️ Practice

Make flashcards for 15 startup metrics and explain each in plain English.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can translate startup-finance language into everyday business language.

LESSON 5

Preparing Finance Meetings

📘 Teaching

Finance meetings are more useful when the right documents, questions, and context are ready. The EA may collect reports, confirm attendees, organize the agenda, and track decisions.

💼 CEO Scenario

A quarterly finance review might need current P&L, cash position, forecast, hiring plan, and questions for leadership.

✍️ Practice

Create a preparation checklist for a quarterly finance review.

🎯 Mastery Standard

The CEO enters finance discussions prepared.

WEEK 10

Confidentiality, Data, Legal, and Risk

Build safe habits for sensitive information, contracts, personnel data, credentials, and high-risk communications.

LESSON 1

Confidentiality as a Daily Practice

📘 Teaching

Confidentiality is about controlling who can access information, where it is stored, how it is shared, and what you say about it. It is not only an NDA.

💼 CEO Scenario

A draft acquisition document should not live in a public folder simply because it is convenient.

✍️ Practice

Audit a fictional workspace for confidentiality risks and list 15 controls.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can identify common information-handling risks.

LESSON 2

NDA and Privacy

📘 Teaching

An NDA governs confidential information by contract. Privacy rules govern personal information. They overlap but are not the same.

💼 CEO Scenario

Employee addresses, payroll details, candidate information, and private records should not be casually shared in general channels.

✍️ Practice

Create a classification scheme: public, internal, confidential, highly confidential. Classify 20 examples.

🎯 Mastery Standard

You can recognize information needing stronger controls.

LESSON 3

Credential and Account Security

📘 Teaching

Use approved delegated access, password managers, device controls, and multi-factor authentication. Never store passwords in spreadsheets or chat.

💼 CEO Scenario

If a platform supports delegation, use that feature rather than collecting a plain-text password.

✍️ Practice

Write a secure account-delegation SOP for email, calendar, and project tools.

🎯 Mastery Standard

Top priorities • calendar highlights • decisions needed • overdue follow-ups • important messages • travel/logistics • risks • next update time

Inbox Triage — The Complete Method

Step 1: Scan. Look at sender, subject, urgency, attachments, deadlines, and whether the CEO personally needs to act.

Step 2: Classify. Use categories such as CEO Action, Delegate, Reply on Behalf, Schedule, Waiting For, FYI, Archive, and Unsubscribe/Filter.

Step 3: Decide the next action. Every important email should end with a visible next action, owner, and date.

Step 4: Protect the CEO. Batch routine updates, route operational questions to the correct owner, and escalate material risks.

Step 5: Close the loop. Track promised replies and follow-ups instead of assuming the sender will remember.

CC — Carbon Copy

CC is used when someone needs visibility but is not the primary person responsible for the action. The recipient can see the other people on the To and CC lines.

Example: To: Vendor • CC: CEO. The vendor owns the response; the CEO is included for visibility.

EA rule: Do not CC the CEO just to make an email look important. Use CC when the CEO genuinely needs visibility, context, or accountability.

BCC — Blind Carbon Copy

BCC hides that recipient from the other recipients' visible To/CC fields. It can be useful for legitimate privacy-sensitive distribution or mailing lists, but it can also create confusion if used secretly in one-to-one business communication.

Example: A large external distribution may use BCC so recipients do not see everyone else's addresses. Always follow company privacy and email policy.

EA rule: Avoid using BCC as a covert way to copy the CEO into a sensitive conversation unless company practice explicitly supports it. Consider whether the recipient would reasonably expect the information to be shared.

To vs CC vs BCC — Quick Decision Guide

FieldUse it whenAsk yourself
ToThe person is expected to act, answer, approve, or decide.“Is an action expected from this person?”
CCThe person needs awareness or context but is not the main action owner.“Does this person need visibility?”
BCCRecipients need privacy from one another, or your policy specifically calls for hidden distribution.“Is there a legitimate privacy reason to hide recipients?”

Meeting Minutes — Professional Format

Meeting: [Title] • Date: [Date/time zone] • Purpose: [Why this meeting exists] • Attendees: [Names/roles]

Decisions: What was decided, by whom, and when.

Action Items: Owner + action + deadline.

Open Questions: What remains unresolved.

Risks/Issues: What could prevent the intended outcome.

Next Checkpoint: Date and expected outcome.

Important: Good minutes are not a word-for-word transcript. They are a reliable record of decisions and commitments.

Rollup: summarizes related information. Example: a project page can show how many open tasks remain.

6. Automation Blueprint

For every automation, design: Trigger → Data → Condition → Action → Human Review → Exception → Log → Measurement.

Workflow A: Meeting → Minutes → Tasks

Calendar event ends → meeting notes/template opens → AI drafts summary from approved notes → EA verifies decisions/names/deadlines → approved action items become Notion tasks → owners are notified → next checkpoint is scheduled.

Workflow B: Important Email → Executive Task

Incoming email matches agreed criteria → create a Notion/PM task with sender, subject, deadline and link → assign category → EA reviews priority → CEO is interrupted only when escalation rules are met.

Workflow C: Waiting For

Action is marked Waiting For → follow-up date is calculated → reminder appears when due → EA sends approved follow-up → status becomes Responded, Escalated, or Closed.

Workflow D: Travel

Trip record created → checklist generated → travel documents linked → time-zone review → calendar blocks created → pre-trip brief generated → contingency items checked → post-trip expense task created.

Workflow E: Board Meeting

Board date selected → preparation project created → tasks generated by relative deadlines → document requests sent → received documents logged → QA checkpoint → CEO briefing prepared → final pack approved → decisions/actions logged after meeting.

8. The EA Automation Safety Rules

Rule 1: Automate repetitive work, not judgment.

Rule 2: Keep human approval for sensitive external communication and consequential decisions.

Rule 3: Never create a secret source of truth. Every automated record should have a clear authoritative source.

Rule 4: Design exception handling before turning the automation on.

Rule 5: Log important automated actions so errors can be investigated.

Rule 6: Minimize confidential data passed between systems and follow company security rules.

Rule 7: Test with dummy information before using real executive data.
